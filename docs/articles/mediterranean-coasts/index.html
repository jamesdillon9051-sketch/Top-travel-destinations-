Choosing a Mediterranean Coast

Seven stretches of coastline across France, Spain, Italy and Turkey, and how to work out which one you actually want.

The Mediterranean coasts in this guide are not interchangeable. Some have sand and some have rock; some are glamorous and some are cheap; two of them are effectively closed for half the year. This is a comparison by the things that decide whether a coastal week works — swimming, crowds, cost, transport and what there is to do when you get out of the water.

Five questions that separate them

Every Mediterranean coast photographs the same way — blue water, a town on a hill, a boat. The differences that matter on a real trip are less photogenic, and there are five of them.

Sand or rock. This decides more holidays than anything else. The Amalfi Coast, the Cinque Terre and much of the Turkish coast are rock platforms, pebble and slipways; Puglia's Ionian side, Mallorca and parts of the French Riviera have real sand. Neither is better, but arriving expecting one and finding the other ruins a week.

How you get around. Some coasts have a train running along them — the Cinque Terre, the Riviera, the Ligurian and Costa Brava lines — which changes everything. Others have a single-lane corniche road shared with coaches, or need a hire car and a ferry.

How long the season lasts. The Amalfi Coast and the Cinque Terre largely close from November to Easter. Turkey's Turquoise Coast is mild enough to visit year-round even when the resorts shut. The Costa del Sol and the Canaries function all winter.

What is behind the beach. A week of swimming needs something to do on the third afternoon. Ruins, a city, mountains or wine make a coastal week; a strip of hotels does not.

Price. The spread across these coasts is roughly threefold for the same standard of accommodation, which is a bigger difference than most people assume when they are choosing between them.

Worth knowing: If you take one thing from this article: check whether your chosen coast has sand. It is the single most common mismatch between expectation and reality.

Seven coasts, compared

  • The Amalfi Coast, Italy — drama, at a price

    The most spectacular of the seven and the least practical: towns stacked on cliffs, no flat ground, a single-lane corniche that gridlocks in summer and almost no sand. Positano, Amalfi and Ravello are extraordinary and expensive, and the season runs Easter to October only.

    Tip Travel by ferry rather than road, split your nights between two towns, and check how many steps your hotel involves before booking.

  • The Turquoise Coast, Turkey — the best value by far

    Eight hundred kilometres of pine-backed coves between Fethiye and Antalya, with Lycian rock tombs above the water, gulet cruises, the 540-km Lycian Way and prices roughly half those of Italy or France.

    Tip Combine a few days on a wooden gulet with a base ashore. The coast road between Fethiye and Antalya is one of the Mediterranean's great drives.

  • The Cinque Terre, Italy — walking between villages

    Five villages on eleven kilometres of Ligurian cliff, connected by a train every twenty minutes and by footpaths through terraced vineyards. Compact, walkable and under real pressure — four million visitors a year to a resident population of about four thousand.

    Tip Sleep in a village and walk before nine. Only Monterosso has real sand; everywhere else you swim off rocks.

  • The French Riviera — cities, art and organisation

    Nice, Antibes, Villefranche and Menton, with a railway along the shore, an international airport in the middle, and more twentieth-century art than any comparable stretch of coast — Matisse, Chagall, Picasso and the Fondation Maeght.

    Tip The best-connected coast of the seven: you can do it entirely by train, and Nice works as a city break in its own right.

  • The Balearics and the Costa Brava, Spain — sand and coves

    Mallorca's Serra de Tramuntana on one side and quiet calas on the other, and the Costa Brava's pine-backed inlets north of Barcelona. Genuine sand, warm shallow water, and a very short, very intense August.

    Tip Go in late September. The sea is at its warmest, the prices halve and the roads empty.

  • Puglia and the Salento, Italy — two coasts, twenty minutes apart

    In the heel, the Adriatic side is limestone cliffs, sea caves and rock platforms, and the Ionian side is long shallow sand. Add whitewashed hill towns, Baroque Lecce and trulli and it is the best-value coastal week in Italy.

    Tip Avoid August, when Italian holidaymakers fill the Salento entirely. May, June and September are empty by comparison.

  • The Aegean, Turkey — ruins and a coastline together

    The stretch around Bodrum and down towards Fethiye combines a proper coastline with the densest concentration of Greek and Roman archaeology anywhere — Ephesus, Priene, Didyma and Aphrodisias are all within reach.

    Tip The Bodrum peninsula is rock and wooden decks rather than beach. If you want sand, look south.

Season is more important than destination

Across all seven, the same pattern holds: June and September are better than July and August in every measurable way. The sea is warmest in September, not August, because it takes the whole summer to heat up. Prices in the first half of September are typically 30–50% below the first half of August. And the crowds fall away sharply after the first week of September, when European schools go back.

May and early June are the other window. The water is cooler — genuinely cold before late May in Liguria and the northern Adriatic, warmer earlier in Turkey and southern Italy — but the walking is at its best, the landscape is green, and everything has reopened for the season.

Winter divides them. The Amalfi Coast and the Cinque Terre largely shut: hotels close, ferries stop and Positano becomes a village of a few hundred people. The French Riviera and the Turkish coast keep working — Antalya averages 15–17°C in January with a great deal of sun, and its archaeological sites are empty. Nothing is swimmable, but a February week of ruins and long lunches is a legitimate trip.

The one coast that inverts this is the archaeological one. If your week is really about Ephesus, Pompeii or the Lycian sites rather than the water, high summer is actively unwise: these are unshaded sites where midday temperatures routinely exceed 38°C.

Worth knowing: The sea is warmest in September and the crowds are gone by the second week. If you can move a coastal trip by one month, move it later.

So which one?

  • You want sand and shallow water

    Puglia's Ionian coast or Mallorca. Both have long, genuinely sandy beaches with shallow entry, which the Amalfi Coast and the Cinque Terre simply do not.

  • You want the best photographs of your life

    The Amalfi Coast, and accept the price and the traffic. Nothing else on this list looks like it, and the arrival into Positano by boat is the best on the Mediterranean.

  • You are travelling without a car

    The French Riviera or the Cinque Terre. Both have a train running along the shore stopping in every town, which makes a car pointless rather than merely inconvenient.

  • Budget is the main constraint

    Turkey's Turquoise Coast, which is roughly half the cost of the Italian and French options for equivalent quality, then Puglia.

  • You want ruins with the swimming

    The Turkish Aegean or the Lycian coast, where a morning at Ephesus or a Lycian hillside city and an afternoon in the sea is the standard day.

  • You want to walk as much as swim

    The Cinque Terre for short village-to-village paths, or the Lycian Way in Turkey for something far more serious. Both are best in spring and autumn, not summer.

The Amalfi Coast

Campania, Italy

A UNESCO-listed stretch of Campanian cliff with lemon terraces, ceramic-domed churches, a Norman-Arab cathedral at Amalfi and Ravello's gardens hanging over the sea.

Don't miss The Path of the Gods — a ridge trail 600 metres above the water, looking down on Positano and across to Capri.

Fethiye & Ölüdeniz

Muğla, Turquoise Coast, Turkey

The base for the Turquoise Coast: gulet trips from the harbour, a ghost village in the hills, an 18-kilometre gorge inland, and the start of the 540-kilometre Lycian Way.

Don't miss Paragliding off Babadağ at 1,965 metres and spiralling down for half an hour onto Ölüdeniz beach.

Nice & the French Riviera

Alpes-Maritimes, Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur, France

France's fifth city curves around a two-mile bay of blue water, with an old town that was Italian until 1860 and a local rail line linking a dozen coastal villages.

Don't miss The Cours Saleya flower market, then the coastal train to Villefranche, Èze and Menton for €2 a hop.
